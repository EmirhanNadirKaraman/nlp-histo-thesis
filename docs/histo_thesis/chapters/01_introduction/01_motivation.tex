\section{Motivation}\label{section:intro-motivation}

Biomedical knowledge is produced faster than any individual can read it, and much of it remains in text, tables, and figures of full-text articles, rather than in a structured, machine-readable form. Open repositories such as PubMed Central make large collections of the literature available as text~\parencite{nlm_pmc_open_access}, so large-scale knowledge extraction is feasible in principle. However, in practice, diagnostically relevant facts, such as a tissue's biomarker expressions, the morphological features that support a diagnosis, or whether a finding is associated with better or worse prognosis, remain scattered across the papers. 

Large language models (LLMs) make the automatic extraction of these findings from biomedical and clinical text more realistic~\parencite{}: a capable LLM can return typed claims with subject and outcome entities, as well as relations between them, more flexibly than traditional hand-built systems. This capability of LLMs makes it feasible to build a clinical knowledge base over the histopathology literature. 

The same models, however, impose risks that a clinical knowledge base cannot tolerate: they might hallucinate claims that seem to be correct at first glance, but are actually unsupported; omit important evidence, disregard nuance, or paraphrase claims in a way that makes verification difficult~\parencite{}. Other work on this topic argues that the knowledge an LLM extracts should be attributed, meaning that each claim should be tied to the evidence supporting it, so it can be verified later by an expert~\parencite{}. 

This thesis therefore treats auditability as a hard requirement rather than a feature added afterward: each claim it produces should carry a pointer back to its supporting evidence. The intended output is a structured clinical knowledge base, whose claims an expert can audit.
