\subsection{Cost-quality analysis}\label{subsec:abc-cost-quality}

The cascade is evaluated on two axes: extraction quality and per-chunk cost. Extraction quality is measured by comparing the final output of the cascade to the silver findings, which are defined in Subsection~\ref{subsec:eval-knowledge-extraction}. Cost is measured by analyzing voter call and escalation statistics, weighted by a manually compiled model price book compiled from providers' pricing pages. A chunk accepted at L1 incurs only the cheap tier cost; a chunk escalated to L2 incurs L2-tier costs on top, and another escalation to L3 causes an extra cost for the API call of the strongest model. Therefore, the cost savings the cascade can deliver, compared to sending every chunk to the strongest model, depend directly on the L1 and L2 escalation rates.

Three operating points are selected from the set of non-dominated configurations, to summarize the cost--quality frontier: the \texttt{economy} point follows the $\varepsilon$-constraint method~\parencite{haimes1971onabicriterionformulation}, and chooses the lowest-cost configuration that satisfies a minimum strict-F1 requirement; the \texttt{knee} point balances quality and cost, and is selected using weighted-sum scalarization~\parencite{boyd2004convexoptimization}, which maximizes $\text{strict-F1} - \lambda\cdot\text{cost}$, where $\lambda$ is the cost-weight coefficient. The \texttt{quality} point is simply the configuration with the highest strict-F1 score. Movement along the cost-quality frontier is largely controlled by the agreement threshold $\theta$, the confidence gate of the cascade's deferral rule~\parencite{jitkrittum2023confidencebasedcascadedeferral}: with inter-voter agreement as the confidence signal, a chunk is accepted when voter agreement exceeds $\theta$, and is escalated or rejected otherwise. Increasing $\theta$ therefore escalates more chunks, and moves the operating point toward higher cost and quality.

This subsection states a design property, rather than a result: agreement-based cascading saves costs only when a sufficient percentage of cases are accepted in the lower tiers. If the L1 and L2 voters often disagree because the voter pools are weak, the task is difficult, or the acceptance threshold is too conservative, the cascade tends to call voters in higher tiers. API calls to the strongest model, plus the overhead of the cheaper voters, are strictly more expensive than if there were no cascade mechanism, and every chunk was sent to the strongest model. This is a property of the cascade architecture, not a failure of a particular implementation. The empirical cost-quality outcome of the calibrated configuration, including a direct comparison to the single strongest model alone, which answers whether the cascade is worth its cost, is reported in Section~\ref{section:results-cost-quality-comparison} and discussed in Section~\ref{section:disc-agreement-based-cascading}.
