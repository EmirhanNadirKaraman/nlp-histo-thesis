\section{Grounding, polarity conflicts, and safety gates}\label{section:disc-grounding-polarity-conflicts-and-safety-gates}

The grounding filter, the polarity hard-fail, and the single-voter escalation gate have the same design intent: to enforce a property that the silver metric does not measure, at the expense of a lower strict-F1, or a small additional cost. Since the output is a clinical knowledge base, these filters and gates should be interpreted as safeguards to ensure that unsupported or polarity-ambiguous claims are filtered out as much as possible, since retaining such claims is more costly than dropping correct ones.

\begin{itemize}[label={},leftmargin=1.5em]

    \item \textbf{The grounding filter enforces faithfulness, rather than optimizing strict-F1.} The highest strict-F1 is obtained when the grounding filter is disabled. Enabling the grounding filter at $0.5$ lowers the strict-F1 from $0.7160$ to $0.652$, which is a deliberate design decision that sacrifices roughly six F1 points (§\ref{section:results-nli-in-grounding-and-relation-classification}). This trade-off is justified because strict-F1 measures agreement with the Opus silver labeler; it does not measure how well each claim is supported by the evidence it cites. These two properties are independent: when the grounding filter is enabled, the silver precision barely moves ($0.875$ with the threshold set to $0.5$, $0.872$ when the filter is disabled), yet roughly $16\%$ of the findings are filtered out; $86\%$ of these filtered-out findings agree with the silver labeler, but are not supported by their cited evidence. 
    
    The bimodal score distribution in Figure~\ref{fig:results-grounding-hist} shows that $77.5\%$ of findings score $\geq 0.9$, whereas roughly $14\%$ score $<0.3$, making the threshold cut-off low-risk: the threshold removes a low-support group, rather than slicing through a dense ambiguous region. Therefore, the resulting strict-F1 of $0.652$ and the six-point F1 cost should be read as the cost of enforcing source faithfulness.
    
    \item \textbf{The polarity hard-fail is a safety net, which did not fire.} Based on the ablation, the polarity hard-fail safety gate, which escalates a chunk when voters disagree on claim polarity, had no measurable effect on the corpus (§\ref{section:results-abc-calibration}). This result should be interpreted as evidence that the evaluation corpus did not contain the polarity-conflict failures this gate is designed to catch. The cost of such an error is high -- the meaning of a clinical claim is silently reversed -- and the gate is almost free. Therefore, the gate is kept enabled for insurance, as a defense mechanism. The thesis does not claim that a benefit is measured; it only claims that its presence is low-cost and justified.
    
    \item \textbf{The single-voter gate is a conservative design choice, not a significant quality gain.} Setting the single-voter gate to \texttt{escalate} rather than \texttt{keep} when there is only one valid voter output for a chunk raises strict-F1 from $0.7147$ to $0.7160$, but this difference falls within the bootstrap confidence interval of Section~\ref{section:results-cost-quality-comparison}. Therefore, it is a design decision to choose \texttt{escalate} for the gate, where no statistically significant improvement was observed. The rationale for choosing \texttt{escalate} is structural: the best-performing voter subset drops Haiku from Level-2 voters, leaving only two voters at that tier; therefore, lone-voter situations at that tier become more common. \texttt{escalate} sends such chunks to the next tier (Sonnet) rather than trusting a single mid-tier voter. As with the grounding filter and the polarity hard-fail, the gate is designed to be conservative on hard cases rather than to maximize strict-F1.

\end{itemize}
