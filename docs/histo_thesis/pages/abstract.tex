\chapter{\abstractname}

Much of the diagnostic knowledge in histopathology literature is in unstructured form: it appears in narrative prose, tables, figures, captions, and footnotes, rather than in queryable databases. Extracting that knowledge at scale is therefore useful, but difficult. The components that appear in scientific PDFs must first be separated from each other and reconstructed into reliable text, table, and figure objects. Large language models can then extract structured claims, but they may hallucinate, omit evidence, fail to attach their outputs to a verifiable source, and quickly become expensive when the best models are used to process every paper.

This thesis presents an end-to-end pipeline that converts open-access histopathology papers into a structured, auditable knowledge base where every figure and table can be traced back to its source page and coordinates, and every extracted rule to the sentence and source paper from which it was derived. The first stage is a hybrid document-extraction pipeline that combines a document-layout model with a table-detection transformer, footnote expansion, caption matching, and a filtering stage to remove text invisible to a human reader. The output of this stage is fed into a seven-stage knowledge-extraction pipeline (MAP $\rightarrow$ grounding $\rightarrow$ normalization $\rightarrow$ grouping $\rightarrow$ canonicalization $\rightarrow$ relation detection $\rightarrow$ resolution). Only the MAP stage is stochastic; once its output is fixed, the downstream stages are deterministic. Every emitted claim carries its source evidence and a natural-language-inference grounding score.

The MAP stage is where the main cost-quality trade-off arises. Instead of running the strongest LLM for each text chunk, the thesis uses agreement-based cascading (ABC): each chunk is routed through a multi-provider ensemble of voters, differing in strength and pricing, and only chunks with insufficient agreement among voters are escalated to more expensive model tiers. 

Provenance is preserved across the full ingested corpus of $977$ papers. On a human-labeled rubric set, the document-extraction stage achieves strict-F1 scores of $0.840$ on figures and $0.838$ on tables, up from the off-the-shelf baselines of $0.447$ and $0.366$. The calibrated cascade reaches $0.716$ strict-F1 on the silver-labeled knowledge-extraction evaluation -- statistically indistinguishable from the best single-model baseline that costs $24\%$ less, while beating every other single model -- and generalizes to a held-out set without meaningful overfitting. Therefore, the cascade is not cost-justified against the strong model on quality alone, and its defensible value lies in its provider independence.

The lasting contribution of the thesis is the entire framework that prioritizes provenance, treats cost and quality as explicit knobs that can be calibrated, and surfaces rule candidates for a domain expert to validate.
